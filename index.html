About the compiler الإمام النووي

Imam Muḥyī al-Dīn Abū Zakariyyā Yaḥyā ibn Sharaf al-Nawawī was born in the village of Nawā near Damascus in 631 A.H. (1233 C.E.). At nineteen he went to Damascus, then the center of learning, and studied under more than twenty leading scholars of hadith, jurisprudence and Arabic. Known for piety, simplicity and a remarkable memory, he wrote enduring works including Riyāḍ al-Ṣāliḥīn, Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, al-Adhkār and al-Minhāj. He died in Nawā in 676 A.H. (1278 C.E.).

His collection of forty (in fact forty-two) hadith has been accepted and treasured by Muslim scholars for over seven centuries because it gathers the core principles of the religion — belief, worship, ethics and fiqh — in a small, memorizable set. The commentary in this workbook is by Dr. Jamal Ahmed Badi.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

This hadith was said by the Prophet ﷺ at the time when a man emigrated from Makkah to Madinah during the Hijrah for the sake of marrying someone, and not for the sake of Islam. It is considered to be one of the greatest hadiths in Islam.

Al-Imam al-Shāfiʿī said: this hadith is one third of the knowledge of Islam; it is related to about 70 topics of fiqh.

Al-Imam Aḥmad (with reference to al-Shāfiʿī's statement) said: Islam is based on three fundamentals, all of which are among the forty hadith:

  1. Hadith 1 — as stated above.
  2. Hadith 5 — “Whosoever introduces into this affair of ours (i.e. Islam) something that does not belong to it, it is to be rejected.”
  3. Hadith 6 — “Truly, what is lawful is evident, and what is unlawful is evident, and in between the two are matters which are doubtful, which many people do not know…”

These three hadith are agreed upon by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, and can be seen as three criteria to help a Muslim evaluate and judge what he does and says “as an ʿibādah” in daily life:

  • Hadith 1 — to evaluate our internal actions (actions of the heart).
  • Hadith 5 — to evaluate our external actions (actions of the limbs).
  • Hadith 6 — to evaluate our dealings (muʿāmalāt — interaction between people).

Niyyah (intention) has two meanings: (i) the intention before an ʿibādah (e.g. prayer); and (ii) the willingness. The second meaning is what is meant in this hadith.

Lessons

The Prophet ﷺ starts the hadith with the principle (“Actions are judged by intentions”) and then gives three examples. This is his methodology: the examples illustrate the principle so it is easier for people to understand it and to apply it to other, similar situations. The three examples consist of one good intention (migration for the sake of Allah and His Messenger) and two bad ones (migration for worldly gain, or for marriage).

This hadith emphasises ikhlāṣ (sincerity — to be truthful and honest to Allah alone, performing an act solely for Allah's sake, seeking no witness except Allah). Ikhlāṣ is one of the conditions for accepting good deeds. The other condition is that the action be done in accordance with the Sharīʿah, as explained in the fifth hadith.

This can be seen in the shahādah:

  • “I bear witness that there is no god but Allah” is the ikhlāṣ — ensuring we do things for the sake of Allah alone.
  • “I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah” — the Sunnah is the manifestation of the Qurʾān; the Prophet ﷺ is our best model. Following his Sunnah in our ʿibādah, akhlāq (ethics) and muʿāmalāt (dealings) ensures we act in accordance with the Sharīʿah.

Thus the shahādah shows the conditions for accepting a deed: (a) it should be for the sake of Allah, because He alone is worshipped; and (b) it should be in accordance with the Sharīʿah.

To achieve ikhlāṣ we must avoid shirk (associating others with Allah, which causes insincerity). Al-Imam al-Harawī said the root cause of insincerity is self-desire (al-hawā); therefore no action should be done out of self-desire. He lists seven types of self-desire:

  1. To make oneself appear good in the hearts of others.
  2. To seek the praises of others.
  3. To avoid being blamed by others.
  4. To seek the glorification of others.
  5. To seek the wealth/money of others.
  6. To seek the services or love of others.
  7. To seek the help of others for oneself.

Ways to obtain ikhlāṣ:

  • Do righteous deeds — the more good deeds we do and the closer we draw to Allah, the more sincere we become.
  • Seek knowledge (ʿilm) before acting — our deeds should be guided by knowledge so they accord with the Sharīʿah.
  • Do not give false impressions — do not make others believe an action was good when it was not.
  • Al-Imam Aḥmad said: before you do anything, check your intention — ask yourself, “Is this for the sake of Allah?”

Ibn al-Qayyim says any action is subject to three defects:

  1. Being conscious that others are observing our action.
  2. Seeking a return (benefit or reward) for the action.
  3. Being self-satisfied with the action.

Examples: if we reach the masjid early, before the imam, and find a place in the first row (ṣaff), we should not be proud or think ourselves better than others — we should praise Allah for enabling us to pray without difficulty. And after every ṣalāh, we should tell ourselves we could have performed it better, and try to improve next time.

Changing the intention mid-action: Ibn Rajab says that, according to the scholars, if the intention at the end of the action matches its beginning (doing it for the sake of Allah), then any change in the middle is forgiven or does not matter, in shā' Allāh. But if the intention at the end does not match the beginning — i.e. it becomes for other than Allah — then one must repent (tawbah).

Four things that contradict ikhlāṣ:

  1. Maʿṣiyah — committing sins, which weakens our ikhlāṣ.
  2. Shirk — associating others with Allah.
  3. Riyāʾ — performing an ʿibādah to show off to others.
  4. Nifāq — hypocrisy.

Even so, some actions are automatically considered to carry a good intention — for example seeking knowledge in Islam, helping the community, and doing daʿwah.

Some rulings (aḥkām) scholars derived from this hadith:

  • When people “swear by Allah” (saying “Wallāhi”) out of habit, they do not truly intend an oath; it is harmless, though a Muslim should do his best to minimise it.
  • When someone is asked to give an oath, what is judged is his intention when he gives it.
  • Intentions can combine — performing an ʿibādah for Allah while also intending to teach others (as the Prophet ﷺ did on Hajj, performing it for Allah and teaching the Companions).
  • A man may go through the process of divorcing his wife, verbally or in court, but it is his intention that counts.
  • What could look like ghībah (backbiting — saying true but bad things about someone behind his back) may in fact be a joke or a duʿāʾ; the intention determines whether it is ghībah or not.

Conclusion

Our actions are shaped by our intentions, whether good or bad. We should therefore always check our intentions before we do or say anything, making sure the action is for the sake of Allah so that it is accepted and rewarded, in shā' Allāh.

💬 Simple Explanation · بِبَسَاطَة

Think of your intention as the price tag your heart quietly sticks on everything you do. Two people can pray shoulder to shoulder, exact same movements, and walk away with completely different rewards, because Allah reads the tag, not just the motion. Same with the good stuff you post, the help you give, the class you show up to. Before you act, ask your heart one small question: “Who is this really for?”

Heart-check: the last good thing you did today, whose notice were you secretly hoping for?

This hadith establishes that the worth of any action depends on the intention behind it. The very same outward deed can be sincere worship or an empty motion, depending on whether it is done for the sake of Allah. It teaches us to examine our motives before we act, and to keep them for Allah alone.

✅ Quick check — Two people give the exact same amount of charity. One wants to be seen as generous; the other gives quietly for Allah. Are their deeds equal before Allah?
No. The outward act is identical, but each is rewarded according to his intention. The sincere giver's charity is accepted as worship; the show-off has already received what he sought — the people's notice.

التَّقْوِيم · Self-Test

What are the two conditions for any deed to be accepted by Allah?

Sincerity (ikhlāṣ) — doing it for Allah alone — and conformity with the Sharīʿah / the Prophet's ﷺ Sunnah.

الإِخْلَاصُ للهِ تَعَالَى، وَمُوَافَقَةُ الشَّرِيعَةِ (اتِّبَاعُ السُّنَّةِ).
After stating the principle, the Prophet ﷺ gives examples. Why teach with examples?

So an abstract principle becomes concrete and easy to apply to new situations — this was his teaching method.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

Al-Imam Muslim reports that, towards the end of his life, ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar (the son of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb) was told by two people that a new sect had emerged from Iraq, called al-Qadariyyah, who denied al-qadar (destiny). ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar then narrated this hadith — which lists qadar as one of the pillars of īmān — to refute their misconception.

Lessons

The etiquette (adab) of seeking knowledge. The hadith teaches us to be clean and wear clean clothes, to sit properly and close to the teacher, to ask questions for better understanding, and to seek knowledge from a reliable source.

Learning through good questions. Questions should be meaningful — leading to valuable knowledge and good action. Good questions benefit everyone present, so the questioner is also teaching others. When Ibn ʿAbbās was asked how he gained his vast knowledge he said: “with an inquisitive tongue and a contemplating heart.” The Prophet ﷺ himself often began by asking questions to prepare the heart to receive the answer; here he calls Jibrīl “the questioner,” showing his appreciation of good questions. The Qurʾān contains more than 1,200 questions, posed to provoke the mind to reflect.

Qadar on two levels. (i) We believe Allah knew, with His ultimate knowledge, everything His creation would do before it happened, and recorded it in al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ (the Preserved Tablet); (ii) we believe it is by Allah's will that these things occur, whether good or bad. Allah created our willingness and ability — we act only when willing and able — yet we remain responsible for our choices.

Misconceptions about qadar. Many confuse association with causation. What we do is not caused by what is written; rather, what we do matches Allah's ultimate knowledge. Allah is Just (ʿĀdil), so He created this life to test us. Guidance (hidāyah) is a mercy and gift from Allah — He gave us the mind, the fiṭrah that loves truth, the ability to act, and sent Messengers with the Message. Misguidance, however, comes from us: those who turn away do so because they choose to, out of arrogance. “And when they swerved from the right way, Allah let their hearts swerve” [al-Ṣaff 61:5]. Those who never received the Message clearly (Ahl al-Fatrah) are excused.

Dealing with qadar through qadar. Illness is qadar, yet the Prophet ﷺ commanded us to seek a cure — and finding the cure is also qadar. If we lose a job, we look for another. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, travelling with Companions, turned back from a town struck by plague; when asked if he was fleeing the qadar of Allah, he replied: “We flee from one qadar to another qadar.” The Prophet ﷺ said: “Be keen for whatever benefits you, seek the help of Allah, and do not be reckless.” So we must make the effort — we are responsible for what we do.

Qadar is not an excuse. If we drive recklessly and cause a death, we cannot say “it was qadar” to escape responsibility — that is why the courts find us guilty. Abusing qadar to justify sin or neglect of an obligation is wrong. Its correct use as an excuse is only when someone did his utmost yet an unavoidable circumstance beyond his control prevented him (e.g. a student who studied hard but fell ill on exam day).

Islam, Īmān and Iḥsān. Al-Islām here is the outward actions of the limbs (the five pillars); al-Īmān is the inner belief of the heart; al-Iḥsān is the highest level. The first two can overlap, and when either word is used alone it includes all three. If īmān is weak it affects one's Islam (deeds); Abū Ḥanīfah said īmān necessitates action. Some scholars said: the Muslims are strangers among the disbelievers, the believers (muʾminūn) are strangers among the Muslims, and the people of iḥsān (muḥsinūn) are strangers among the believers. Iḥsān — worshipping Allah as though you see Him — flows from loving, fearing, hoping in, and trusting Allah.

“I do not know.” When Jibrīl asked about the Hour, the Prophet ﷺ said neither he nor the questioner knew — teaching us that even the most knowledgeable does not know everything and should admit it. Imam Mālik answered “I don't know — Allah knows better” to most of forty questions, unwilling to risk a wrong answer. A true scholar has the courage to say “I do not know.”

The signs of the Hour mentioned here are minor signs. We believe in them but should not be preoccupied with when they will occur; rather, since some are themselves evils, we should steer clear of those bad deeds.

Conclusion

This hadith contains the whole of the religion: the five pillars of Islam, the articles of īmān (including belief in qadar), the highest level of iḥsān, and the etiquette of seeking and teaching knowledge.

💬 Simple Explanation · بِبَسَاطَة

Picture a stranger walking into class and quizzing the teacher on the whole syllabus in five minutes, then the teacher says: that was actually the exam board, checking you were taught right. That is this hadith. Jibrīl draws out the entire religion in three layers: Islam is what your body does (the five pillars), Iman is what your heart believes (the six articles), and Ihsan is the upgrade, doing it all as if Allah is right there watching, because He is.

If someone watched only your actions today, would they see Islam? If they could read your heart, would they find Iman? And did any of it reach Ihsan?

This hadith summarises the entire religion in three levels. Islam is the outward practice (the five pillars). Iman is the inner belief (the six articles of faith). Ihsan is the highest level: worshipping Allah as though you see Him, knowing that He sees you. It also models the manners of learning, and affirms belief in divine decree.

✅ Quick check — Someone says, 'Everything is already written, so why pray or make any effort?' Where is the flaw?

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

This hadith overlaps with Hadith 2. Most scholars say Imam al-Nawawī still included it — despite the repetition — because of the sheer importance of the five pillars of Islam. It stresses the fundamentals of outward submission to Allah, which are like the pillars of a structure: fulfil them and you have laid a solid foundation for your dīn as a 'home'. The other acts of Islam are fine touches completing that structure. If the pillars are not built, the whole structure may be threatened — and violating the shahādah is the most dangerous of all.

Lessons

The use of metaphor and simile. This hadith pictures the dīn as a building. The Qurʾān and Sunnah use such imagery often: the believer's dīn built on sound foundation vs. the hypocrite's on crumbling ground [al-Tawbah 9:109]; light as guidance in the believer's heart [al-Nūr 24:35]; those who fail their trust likened to a donkey loaded with books it cannot understand [al-Jumuʿah 62:5]; and rain falling on different soils to depict who benefits from the Message. One Companion said he had “seen” Paradise and the Fire — through the eyes of the Prophet ﷺ, trusting the Prophet's ﷺ description more than his own sight. Using the right mode of expression (rational, visual, emotional) is a powerful tool for daʿwah today.

First Pillar — the Shahādah. Its first half (none worthy of worship but Allah) has seven conditions: knowledge, certainty, acceptance, submission/compliance, truthfulness, sincerity, and love. The shahādah is not mere words on the tongue; lived sincerely, it stops us doing anything that contradicts it. Its second half (Muhammad is His Messenger) requires: believing him and all he conveyed, obeying his commands, avoiding what he forbade, emulating him in worship, character and life, loving him above all, and understanding, practising and promoting his Sunnah without causing chaos or harm.

Second Pillar — establishing Ṣalāh. Iqāmat al-ṣalāh is broader than merely 'performing' prayer: doing wudūʾ properly, praying on time, in congregation (27 times the reward), fulfilling its conditions, observing humility and adab, and following its sunnah acts. Allah first commanded fifty prayers, reduced to five (with the reward of fifty); their times, spread through the day, help us manage our affairs and build community solidarity — prayer is not a burden.

Third Pillar — Zakāh. Given in specified amounts, ways and conditions. Learning its details becomes obligatory once a person owns the kind of wealth that requires it (farmers, traders, property owners).

Fourth Pillar — Hajj. Obligatory once in a lifetime for those who meet the conditions (financial means, a safe way to travel). Those who can, should not delay it. Some scholars say if one could perform Hajj several times, it may be better to use that wealth to help others fulfil their obligation or to benefit the community. Many injuries at Hajj (e.g. at the Jamarāt) come from neglecting the adab and sunan — using large stones, ignoring directions, or crowding at peak times.

Fifth Pillar — Fasting. Ramadan is a training program; we should carry its good deeds beyond it — praying in the mosque, tahajjud, reciting the Qurʾān, caring for others. The Prophet ﷺ recommended completing the Qurʾān in a month (a juzʾ a day); if not, even a page or two daily. We should not bind ourselves with commitments the Sharīʿah did not require, lest we give up; better to act with ease, aiming for continuity.

Conclusion

Every pillar of Islam has rulings, conditions and manners (aḥkām wa ādāb). We must learn them and remind ourselves regularly — especially before Ramadan or Hajj — so we perform the pillars properly and in accordance with the Sharīʿah.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

This hadith was recorded by al-Bukhārī, Muslim and others, and narrated by several Companions besides ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd. Different versions carry slightly different wordings which affect the meaning of the stages of creation:

1. The word “nuṭfah.” It is not in al-Bukhārī's or Muslim's narration but was added in others (including al-Nawawī's choice), giving two views:

  • First view (held by the majority): the three stages are forty days each — 120 days total — after which the rūḥ is breathed in and the provision, lifespan, deeds and destiny are recorded. A difficulty is that this reading appears to conflict with modern embryology; and on this view the fatwā allows abortion (with a valid reason such as danger to the mother's life) only before 120 days.
  • Second view: “nuṭfah” is not part of the text, so the three stages occur within the first forty days; the rūḥ is breathed in after forty days. This correlates with scientific findings, and on this view the fatwā allows abortion only before forty days.

2. The last section (“By Allah…”). Some scholars say it is the words of Ibn Masʿūd, not the Prophet ﷺ. It is still accepted because it concerns matters beyond our perception and Ibn Masʿūd likely derived it from another hadith. Related narrations add: “one of you performs the deeds of the people of Paradise as it appears to people” — like the hypocrites, who outwardly resemble the believers but deny the Message in their hearts, and so end in the Fire.

Lessons

Study a matter across many hadith. Do not rely on a single narration; some narrators report by meaning, not exact wording. Compare the texts on an issue for a complete and correct understanding.

Do not despair into fatalism (jabriyyah). Hearing this hadith without explanation, some fear they are of the doomed group and conclude “why bother doing good?” This is wrong. Allah is Just; if we are good to Him and trust Him, He will be good to us. A Companion hoped to be martyred by an arrow through his neck; the Prophet ﷺ said, “If you are honest with Allah, Allah will be honest with you,” and he died exactly as he hoped. The closer we are to Allah, the more He helps and guides us. The frightening last section applies only to a few, such as the hypocrites.

Combine hope with fear. We should worship Allah with equal hope and fear — no despair, and no over-confidence that breeds laziness — together with loving and trusting Him most.

Categories of qadar. (1) al-Qadar al-Kullī, the general decree recorded in al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ; (2) al-Qadar al-Sanawī, the annual decree (Laylat al-Qadar) matching what is written. What is written is known only to Allah; to us it is ghayb. What is written does not cause our actions — our deeds match Allah's ultimate knowledge, showing His glory. We are responsible for what we choose. People have embraced Islam at the last moment and entered Paradise; others turn to Allah in their fifties or sixties and are forgiven.

A miracle of the hadith. It described the stages of the embryo 1,400 years before science confirmed them. (The Qurʾān mentions these stages too, without the time periods.) At a European conference on creation, some attendees embraced Islam when Muslim scholars showed this was documented in the Qurʾān and Sunnah.

The make-up of the human being (to understand qadar rightly): the intellect (al-ʿAql), by which a sane person becomes mukallaf (accountable); the innate nature (al-Fiṭrah), which loves good and hates evil but can be corrupted by environment and upbringing; the pre-creation covenant to worship Allah alone; and willingness (al-Irādah) and power (al-Qudrah), which are neutral and can be turned to good or evil. We are also given desires (shahawāt).

Internal and external challenges. Internally: self-desire (hawā) and the nafs in three states — the nafs that urges evil (ammārah), the self-reproaching nafs (lawwāmah), and the peaceful nafs (muṭmaʾinnah) — which we may pass through within a single hour. Externally: the existing prohibitions that tempt us, and the whispering (waswasah) of Satan, who can only insinuate, dressing up evil as attractive or urging us to delay good.

We still need Allah's tawfīq. Even with all these faculties, without Allah's enabling grace we may be misled. So we must draw near to Him and constantly make duʿāʾ — which is why at least 17 times a day we ask, “Guide us to the Straight Path.” There are two guidances: to the path (becoming Muslim) and within the path (staying and increasing) — and we need the latter more than we need to breathe.

The man on the island. A man worshipped Allah alone for 70 years on an island. At death Allah asked whether he entered Paradise by His mercy or by his deeds; the man said, “by my deeds.” Allah had the bounty of eyesight weighed against 70 years of worship — and the single bounty of sight outweighed them all. If we worshipped Allah for 70 years we still could not repay one of His bounties. As the scholars say: to know Allah's bounty upon you, close your eyes.

Conclusion

The hadiths are the sources of our īmān, knowledge and guidance from the Prophet ﷺ. Studying and understanding them activates our insight (baṣīrah), enlightens the heart, and lifts the soul — keeping us, by Allah's help, on the right path to the end, in shā' Allāh.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

Like Hadith 1, this is one of the most important hadiths; Imam al-Nawawī said every Muslim should memorise it. It is the criterion for judging external actions of worship: an action not done in accordance with the Sharīʿah and the Sunnah is rejected and unaccepted. It complements Hadith 1 (the criterion for the inner intention). Scholars say acceptance of any act of worship rests on two conditions: (i) sincere intention, for Allah alone; and (ii) conformity with the Sunnah of the Prophet ﷺ. See also al-Kahf 18:110, al-Aḥzāb 33:21, and “Say: if you love Allah, follow me, Allah will love you and forgive your sins.”

Lessons

Following the Sunnah. Violating this concept leads to bidʿah (discussed fully in Hadith 28). Scholars divide the Prophet's ﷺ actions into those done as worship (ʿibādah) and those that were not (customs, habitual acts). We are obliged to follow only the first kind, which is known by clear indicators: a command to do or avoid something, a promised reward, or a warned punishment.

Five checks that keep an act of worship in accordance with the Sunnah:

  1. Time — each ʿibādah has its appointed time (the five prayers, Ramadan for fasting, the days of Hajj from 8–12 Dhū al-Ḥijjah).
  2. Place — certain acts have designated places (Hajj rites, iʿtikāf, entering iḥrām). Entering iḥrām in Jeddah, for instance, is incorrect.
  3. Quantity — fixed numbers (rakʿahs, the seven rounds of ṭawāf). Deliberately altering them can make the act liable to rejection.
  4. Way — the manner shown by the Prophet ﷺ: “Pray as you have seen me praying.” (Ṣalāt al-Janāzah, for example, has no rukūʿ or sujūd.) Learn the correct way before performing an act.
  5. Type — if the Sharīʿah specified a type, keep to it (e.g. the animals valid for sacrifice; a fatwā permitting chicken for sacrifice is a violation of type).

Regular vs. occasional acts. What the Prophet ﷺ did only from time to time (e.g. reciting Sūrahs al-Sajdah and al-Insān on some Fridays) we should also do only occasionally — otherwise people will think it obligatory when it is not.

Actions not done as worship. The Prophet ﷺ had long hair, opened his top buttons in heat, wore a turban by the custom of the time, and used a stick — none for the purpose of ʿibādah. We should not turn these into acts of worship; our intention must match his.

Islam is complete. “Today I have perfected your religion for you” [al-Māʾidah 5:3]. Since it is complete, adding to it or deleting from it is an affront to Allah and His Messenger. Al-Shāṭibī warns that treating non-worship as worship leads to bidʿah — as with the three men who vowed never to marry, to pray all night, and to fast every day; the Prophet ﷺ, the most pious of all, did none of these to that extreme.

The religion is built on ease. Al-Shāṭibī: do not attach self-imposed hardship to worship hoping for extra reward. Given a choice, take the easier way — warm water for wudūʾ in the cold, the nearer mosque. The Prophet ﷺ told a man fasting while standing in the sun to complete his fast but to get out of the sun. Choosing ease helps us keep our commitment.

Degrees of rejection. If someone adds something to an act and violates its conditions (e.g. in prayer), the act is not accepted. Any bidʿah is liable to rejection, and the doer may be questioned or punished — though a valid excuse may mean no reward yet no punishment. The same applies to transactions (muʿāmalāt): dealings built on ḥarām principles are rejected. Ibn Rajab notes scholars differ over some cases (praying in silk, or in a wrongfully-taken house): most say the act is valid but the person is answerable for the wrongdoing.

Conclusion

These hadith chosen by al-Nawawī are principles and criteria that make practice easy. Hadith 5 sets the criterion by which a Muslim assesses his actions to ensure their correctness and acceptability.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

Imam al-Bukhārī placed this hadith at the very beginning of the Book of Trading (Kitāb al-Buyūʿ). Al-Bukhārī was not only a master of hadith but a jurist (faqīh), and his fiqh shows in how he titled and arranged his chapters. Starting the Book of Trading with this hadith implies that doubtful matters are especially bound up with what we buy, sell and trade.

Al-Bukhārī was shaped by his father, who said that for forty years he never brought anything doubtful into his home; whatever the family ate, drank, wore or used was ḥalāl, and it is said his duʿāʾ was answered because of it. As a scholar of narrator-criticism (al-jarḥ wa-l-taʿdīl), al-Bukhārī was so cautious and pious that, rather than call a narrator a liar outright, he would say 'he has been labelled a liar.' In contested matters he took the cautious view.

Lessons

Three categories. The vast majority of acts are plainly lawful or plainly unlawful; only a minority are doubtful. The Prophet ﷺ uses two vivid images: doubtful things are like grazing right at the edge of a king's private reserve — the shepherd will soon stray in; and Allah's reserve is what He has forbidden. This shows doubtful matters sit close to the unlawful.

Glorifying Allah. To honour Allah is to honour His commands and prohibitions. A Salaf said: 'Do not look at how small the sin is, but at the greatness of the One you disobeyed.' Even a minor sin is disobedience to Allah, and glorifying Allah is a principle of tawḥīd — a work of the heart, which is why the heart is the hadith's climax.

The heart is the key. Only a sound heart — full of love, fear and glorification of Allah — restrains a person from the doubtful; a diseased, weak-īmān heart drifts through the disliked (makrūh) into the forbidden. Hundreds pray side by side in the masjid; what distinguishes them is the state of the heart. The Prophet ﷺ said Abū Bakr's īmān would outweigh the īmān of the whole ummah, and that when others hesitated at his call, Abū Bakr believed at once — a matter of the heart. Another hadith: trials are shown to the heart again and again; a heart that yields to them gets a black spot, one that resists gets a white spot, until a heart is either pure or sealed.

The methodology of principle. The Prophet ﷺ discusses a concept, then states a principle (usually the closing words) that we can apply to other, similar matters — here, to guard the heart also protects our character (akhlāq) and dealings.

Doubtful matters in daily life. In trade, cheating can be done 'professionally'; in food, ingredients such as lecithin and emulsifiers (the 'E' numbers) may derive from what is unlawful, so we should read labels carefully — for if what we eat is ḥarām, even our duʿāʾ may be unanswered. We should also guard our health against excess preservatives, colourings and chemicals. Some generally-permissible acts (like staying up late for no reason) can slide into neglecting a wājib, and rulings built on weak hadith can themselves be treated as doubtful.

Purifying the heart. To keep the heart full of light and able to sense the doubtful: al-muḥāsabah (self-accountability), al-murāqabah (awareness that Allah watches), and dhikr (remembrance of Allah, which purifies the heart when done regularly, especially after prayers). Also ṣalāh upon the Prophet ﷺ: we should not abbreviate 'ṣallallāhu ʿalayhi wa-sallam' — each time we say it we are rewarded tenfold, and Allah sends light into the heart of the one who does.

Conclusion

A Muslim meets doubtful matters daily; this hadith equips him to handle them. The ongoing preparation is to purify the heart — through modesty, murāqabah, muḥāsabah, dhikr and ṣalāh upon the Prophet ﷺ. 'Behold, in the body there is a piece of flesh; if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt — and behold, it is the heart.'

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

Knowing a hadith's occasion (sabab al-wurūd) helps us understand it. This one was said when the Prophet ﷺ announced, 'Allah has enjoined Hajj upon you, so perform Hajj.' A man asked, 'Every year, O Messenger of Allah?' … until the Prophet ﷺ said: 'Leave me with what I have left to you; whatever I forbid you, avoid it, and whatever I command you, do of it what you can.' Had he answered 'yes,' Hajj could have become an annual obligation on everyone.

Lessons

Ask the right kind of question. Excessive questioning about an obligation can bring complication and hardship. Good questions that lead to knowledge and good action are encouraged (as in Hadith 1); what is discouraged is questioning that breeds confusion, doubt and chaos, or needless detail.

Commands: 'as much as you can.' The Sharīʿah is flexible and practical; capacity is taken into account. Hajj is due when one has the means; if tied up with debts or clashing duties, it may be delayed ('…those who can afford the journey' [Āl ʿImrān 3:97]). In prayer, do it in its best time and mode (congregation), but pray sitting if you cannot stand; in fasting, a traveller or the sick may break the fast and make it up later.

Prohibitions: avoid completely. The forbidden must be avoided entirely — and so must whatever leads to it, even without intending the ḥarām itself. Guarding the approaches keeps us from falling in. There are narrow exceptions understood from the Qurʾān and Sunnah — necessity, or choosing the lesser of two harms (e.g. eating something forbidden to save one's life) — which the scholars call weighing benefits against harms.

Do not attach hardship to worship. Al-Shāṭibī: take the easier lawful option (warm water for wudūʾ in the cold); hardship is not intended by the Sharīʿah. The same applies to recommended acts: do not bind yourself to a strict schedule you cannot keep — 'Take on only what you can sustain, for Allah does not tire but you will,' and 'the deeds most beloved to Allah are the most constant, even if small.'

Conclusion

Understanding these principles helps us live practically and fulfil our obligations rightly, leading us to love, appreciate, and continuously practise worship.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

The word ṭayyib is used throughout the Qurʾān and Sunnah for actions, deeds, people, things and speech; literally it means 'good,' and Ibn Rajab explained it as ṭāhir — pure. 'Allah is ṭayyib' means He has every attribute of perfection, free of any shortcoming or need. 'He accepts only what is ṭayyib' refers to all good deeds: Allah does not accept a deed spoiled by what ruins it — for example, showing off, or (where wealth is involved) wealth from unlawful sources. Allah commanded the believers as He commanded the Messengers: 'O Messengers, eat of the good things' [al-Muʾminūn 23:51]; 'O you who believe, eat of the good things We have provided you' [al-Baqarah 2:172].

Lessons

Lawful earning and consuming are keys to the acceptance of our deeds: the money we earn must be pure and lawful, the food we eat must be ḥalāl, and the money we buy it with must come from lawful sources. It is Allah's right to declare things lawful or unlawful; people who forbid what He permitted, or permit what He forbade, are in the wrong.

Etiquette (adab) of duʿāʾ drawn from this hadith: eat and earn what is lawful; seize the times when duʿāʾ is answered (travel, sickness, prostration, rainfall, the last third of the night); be humble; raise the hands to the sky; and be earnest, repeating 'yā Rabb, yā Rabb.' Duʿāʾ is a high form of worship — we need Allah's mercy more than we need air. If the adab are neglected — above all, unlawful sustenance — the duʿāʾ may go unanswered: as the hadith's traveller, dishevelled and dusty, raises his hands while his food, drink and clothing are all unlawful, 'so how can he be answered?'

Charity from pure sources only. Allah accepts only the pure, so wealth from unlawful sources must not be given as ṣadaqah or used for worship such as Hajj — 'filth does not expiate filth' (Ibn ʿAbbās). Stolen money must be returned to its owner (part of true repentance); if the owner cannot be found, some scholars allow it be spent on public benefit.

Public property (ghulūl). Wrongfully taking what belongs to a company, organisation or institution — office paper and pens, the photocopier, the company car, phone or money without permission — is ghulūl, a grave matter. ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz used the state candle only for state work and switched to his own candle otherwise; we should likewise switch off the lights and air-conditioning when we leave, and be trustworthy with what is allocated for a purpose.

Healthy, pure food. Beyond lawfulness, we should be mindful of ingredients and of health — harmful additives are not 'ṭayyib.'

Conclusion

What we eat shapes our character. Eating the ṭayyib in the right manner (without excess), and giving charity from the ṭayyib, raises our īmān and purifies the heart — so that we ourselves become of the ṭayyibūn, pure and blessed by Allah.

📚 Full Commentary · الشَّرْح

Background

This hadith runs parallel to Hadith 6: the Prophet ﷺ gives a criterion for deciding whether something is sound. Another wording adds: 'Truth is tranquillity and falsehood is doubt' — truth brings the heart to rest, falsehood unsettles it. It lays down a principle applicable to every area of life, and points the way to truth and righteousness.

Lessons

Act on certainty, leave doubt. Do a (permissible) act only when your heart is at peace with it; if something disturbs the heart, leave it. The believer's heart is tranquil before truth and shaky before falsehood.

The criterion has conditions. It works only for the guided Muslim enlightened by revelation (the Qurʾān and Sunnah) and adhering to it — with knowledge, īmān and obedience. For one immersed in the forbidden it will not function reliably: the killers of al-Ḥusayn (the narrator's brother) once debated whether killing a mosquito was permissible, having shed sacred blood.

The cautious approach. Where scholars genuinely differ — whether reciting al-Fātiḥah is obligatory in congregational prayer, or whether zakāh is due on jewellery worn — some scholars take the safer path (recite it; pay the zakāh) so the Muslim is 'saved' either way. Another approach weighs the strength of the evidence and follows the sounder proof. Both existed among the scholars; we need not decide which is superior.

Principles of fiqh here. Certainty takes precedence over mere conjecture: if impurity is somewhere on a garment but you are unsure where, wash the whole; if unsure whether you prayed one rakʿah or two, build on the certain (one). No ijtihād is made where the Qurʾān or an authentic hadith is clear and decisive.

No piety in avoiding the clearly lawful. One is not rewarded for refusing meat as 'righteousness.' In the hadith of the three men — one vowing never to sleep, one to fast always, one never to marry — the Prophet ﷺ was displeased and said: 'This is my way, and whoever turns away from my way is not of me.' Avoiding the lawful for health or another valid reason is fine; treating it as worship is not.

Beware Satan's colouring. A trick of Shayṭān is to dress up the forbidden as permissible. If something is ḥarām it stays ḥarām, whatever the packaging. In modern transactions, hidden ribā can make matters unclear; it is safer to avoid what we are unsure of and to keep informed of scholars' verdicts on contemporary issues (banking, insurance, and the like).

Conclusion

This hadith gives the Muslim a practical criterion for judging doubtful acts — provided it is applied correctly and not hijacked by false perceptions or personal interest.

Background

Before Islam, human life had little value — a person could be killed for revenge, for tribal pride, or a newborn girl buried out of shame. Islam stressed the value and sanctity of life: it may not be taken except lawfully, where a serious violation of the Sharīʿah has occurred, and only by the highest authority (the judge), never for personal interest.

Islam builds a society whose rules make these three cases rare: it regulates relationships to prevent zinā, and spreads knowledge and daʿwah so people understand and hold to their religion. In the Prophet's ﷺ time and that of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs, such cases were very few. Read positively, the hadith declares Muslim blood sacred and treasured. In his Farewell Sermon the Prophet ﷺ re-emphasised this very principle.

Lessons

Punishments are protective measures. What looks like punishment is Islam safeguarding society: promoting chastity and lawful marriage, building a caring community where rich and poor look after one another, which reduces hatred, conflict and killing. Holding firmly to Islam itself — through knowledge and daʿwah — minimises the exceptional cases in the hadith.

The three cases in which a Muslim's blood may lawfully be shed, by due process: the married adulterer (al-thayyib al-zānī); a life for a life (retribution for murder); and one who forsakes his religion, abandoning the community.

The modern challenge. Media, the internet and entertainment are often misused to normalise the very three evils named here — adultery, violence, and apostasy — presenting bad as good and good as bad. Leaders, callers, teachers and parents must study why divorce, adultery, violence and apostasy are rising, and revive their roles, especially in protecting the minds and character (akhlāq) of the youth. Studies on the negative influence of television (e.g. Jerry Mander; Zig Ziglar) can be learned from.

Community responsibility. Where Muslims neglect one another — in education, health, guidance, finance and care — others step in and some families end up forsaking the faith; here the apostasy is partly the community's own fault for not looking out for each other. Mind-manipulation by hostile narratives also produces double-standard, identity-weak Muslims. We should keep our Islamic identity while benefiting from genuine progress, and move from theory to real strategies that help Muslims be confident and proud of who they are.

Conclusion

Read this hadith in its positive light: the emphasis is the value of human life, not the punishments. Islam values life and chastity (ʿiffah, ṭahārah) and puts in place a system to minimise the three cases. Opponents twist it to accuse Islam of barbarity; our task is to uphold its principles — the sanctity of life, that killing is a crime, and the honour of chastity — and to reduce their violation.

Life is sacred by defaultدَمُ المُسْلِمِ مَعْصُومٌ، وَنُدْرَةُ الاسْتِثْنَاءِ تُبَيِّنُ عِظَمَ حُرْمَتِهِ.damu l-muslimi maʿṣūm, wa-nudratu l-istithnāʾi tubayyinu ʿiẓama ḥurmatih.A Muslim's blood is inviolable; the three exceptions are so narrow and rare that the hadith actually proclaims how treasured life is.
The three cases, by due authorityالثَّلَاثُ: الثَّيِّبُ الزَّانِي، وَالنَّفْسُ بِالنَّفْسِ، وَالمُرْتَدُّ المُفَارِقُ لِلْجَمَاعَةِ؛ بِحُكْمِ القَاضِي لَا آحَادِ النَّاسِ.ath-thalāth: ath-thayyibu z-zānī, wa-n-nafsu bi-n-nafs, wa-l-murtaddu l-mufāriqu li-l-jamāʿah; bi-ḥukmi l-qāḍī lā āḥādi n-nās.The married adulterer; a life for a life (murder); and the one who forsakes his religion and abandons the community — and only through the judgement of lawful authority, never private hands.
ḥurmah (ح ر م)الحُرْمَةُ: العِصْمَةُ، فَدَمُ المُسْلِمِ مَحْمِيٌّ كَحُرْمَةِ المُحَرَّمَاتِ.al-ḥurmah: al-ʿiṣmah, fa-damu l-muslimi maḥmiyyun ka-ḥurmati l-muḥarramāt.From the root of ḥarām — inviolability. The same root that names the forbidden also names what is sacred and protected, like Muslim blood.
Read it positivelyالعِبْرَةُ حِفْظُ النَّفْسِ لَا العُقُوبَةُ، وَقَدْ شَرَعَ الإِسْلَامُ مَا يُقَلِّلُ هَذِهِ الحَالَاتِ.al-ʿibratu ḥifẓu n-nafsi lā l-ʿuqūbah, wa-qad sharaʿa l-islāmu mā yuqallilu hādhihi l-ḥālāt.Islam builds marriage, justice and a caring community precisely so these exceptions almost never arise. The emphasis is protection of life, not punishment.
✅ Quick check — A critic says this hadith shows Islam is 'bloodthirsty.' How does the hadith itself answer that?
It says the opposite: a Muslim's blood is unlawful to shed except in three narrow cases decided by lawful authority. By making the exceptions so rare, it underscores the sanctity of life — and Islam builds a whole social system to keep those cases from arising.

التَّقْوِيم · Self-Test

What are the three cases in which a Muslim's blood may lawfully be shed?

The married person who commits adultery; a life for a life (retribution for murder); and one who forsakes his religion and abandons the community.

الثَّيِّبُ الزَّانِي، وَالنَّفْسُ بِالنَّفْسِ، وَالتَّارِكُ لِدِينِهِ الْمُفَارِقُ لِلْجَمَاعَةِ.
Why should this hadith be read 'positively'?

Because its point is the sanctity of human life: the exceptions are so few and controlled that they prove how valued and protected Muslim blood is.

لِأَنَّ مَقْصُودَهُ حُرْمَةُ النَّفْسِ، وَنُدْرَةُ الِاسْتِثْنَاءَاتِ تُبَيِّنُ عِظَمَ قَدْرِ دَمِ الْمُسْلِمِ.
Who has the right to carry out these cases?

Only the lawful authority (the judge), never individuals acting on their own — to prevent abuse.

وَلِيُّ الْأَمْرِ (الْقَاضِي) وَحْدَهُ، لَا آحَادُ النَّاسِ، مَنْعًا لِلتَّعَدِّي.
